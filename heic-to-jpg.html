What HEIC actually is

HEIC is the file format iPhones have used for photos since iOS 11 in 2017. The name stands for High Efficiency Image Container, and the point of it is size: a HEIC photo is usually around half the size of the same photo saved as JPG, with no visible difference. On a phone holding twenty thousand photos, that adds up to a lot of storage.

The catch is that Apple adopted it years before the rest of the industry caught up. So the format is excellent, and almost nothing outside Apple's own software can open it.

You may also see .heif. HEIF is the broader standard and HEIC is Apple's specific flavour of it. For practical purposes they are the same thing, and this converter handles both.

Why Windows and most websites reject HEIC files

Windows 10 and 11 can display HEIC, but only if you install Microsoft's HEIF Image Extensions from the Microsoft Store, and the codec half of that used to cost money. Most people never install it, so the file simply refuses to open and Windows offers no useful explanation.

Websites are a separate problem. Upload forms usually accept a fixed list of formats, and that list was written before HEIC existed. Job application portals, insurance claim uploads, print shops, government forms, and most content management systems will reject a HEIC file outright or accept it and then fail to generate a thumbnail.

Older versions of Photoshop, most Android phones, and a lot of email clients have the same gap. Converting to JPG sidesteps all of it in one step, because JPG is the closest thing the internet has to a universal format.

Does converting to JPG lose quality?

Technically yes, but in practice you will not see it.

Both HEIC and JPG are lossy formats, meaning both discard some data to save space. Converting from one to the other means the image is decoded and then re-encoded, and that second encode throws away a little more. This is called generation loss.

At the default quality setting of 88% the result is visually identical to the original for normal viewing, printing and sharing. If you are archiving photos you care about, two suggestions:

  • Push quality to 95 or 100 before converting. The file gets bigger, but the loss becomes genuinely undetectable.
  • Keep the original HEIC files. Convert copies. There is no way to recover data once it has been discarded, so the original is your safety net.

If you need zero loss, choose PNG instead of JPG in the format dropdown. PNG is lossless, so nothing is discarded during the re-encode. The trade-off is file size, typically five to ten times larger than the JPG.

Worth knowing

Live Photos are stored as a HEIC still plus a separate short video file. Converting the HEIC gives you the still frame only. The motion is in the paired video, and no image converter can extract it.

How to make your iPhone shoot JPG in the first place

If you convert HEIC files often, stop the problem at the source. On your iPhone:

  1. Open Settings
  2. Tap Camera
  3. Tap Formats
  4. Choose Most Compatible

From then on the camera saves JPG. Photos already on the phone stay HEIC, so you will still need to convert those, but new ones are handled.

The cost is storage. Expect your photos to take roughly twice the space. There is also a second setting worth knowing about, under Settings → Photos → Transfer to Mac or PC. Setting that to Automatic makes the phone convert photos to JPG as it transfers them over a cable, which solves the problem for anything you move off the phone by wire.

Why your iPhone photos are HEIC in the first place

Apple switched the default camera format in 2017, with iOS 11. Before that every photo was a JPG. Since then, unless you have changed a setting, your iPhone saves photos as HEIC , High Efficiency Image Container, a wrapper around the HEIF standard.

The reason is storage. HEIC uses a much newer compression method than JPG, one derived from modern video codecs. In practice a HEIC photo takes roughly half the space of the same photo saved as JPG, at similar visible quality. On a phone holding several thousand photos, that difference is measured in gigabytes.

The format also does things JPG cannot. It stores 16-bit colour instead of 8-bit, which matters for HDR shots. It can hold several images in one file, which is how Live Photos and burst sequences work. It keeps depth maps from Portrait mode, which is what lets you re-edit the background blur later. None of that survives a conversion to JPG.

So why can't anything open them?

Because support outside Apple's ecosystem is still patchy, eight years on. The usual places it breaks:

  • Windows , Windows 11 handles HEIC in Photos, but the older Windows 10 install often needs a codec from the Microsoft Store, and on some machines that codec is a paid add-on.
  • Older editing software , Photoshop supports it in recent versions; a copy from a few years ago will not.
  • Websites and web forms , this is where most people hit the wall. Uploading ID to a bank, a photo to an insurance claim, an image to a listing site. Most upload forms accept JPG and PNG and silently reject anything else.
  • Email recipients , you send a photo, the person on the other end gets a file their computer treats as unknown.
  • Print services , many photo labs still want JPG.

The frustrating part is that it usually works fine right up until it doesn't. AirDrop between Apple devices converts automatically, so the problem only appears when a file crosses over to Windows, Android or a web form.

Does converting lose quality?

Slightly, and it is worth understanding why. HEIC is already a lossy format, so the photo has been compressed once. Converting to JPG decompresses it and compresses it again using a different, older method , a second generation of loss.

At quality 85 to 95 the difference is invisible in normal viewing. You would need to zoom well past 100% on a detailed area to spot it. What you will lose without ambiguity is the extras: HDR range beyond 8-bit, depth data from Portrait mode, and the extra frames in a Live Photo. If any of that matters, keep the HEIC original and treat the JPG as a copy for sharing.

The JPG will also be larger than the HEIC it came from, often around double. That is the storage advantage of HEIC working in reverse.

HEIC and HEIF , is there a difference?

HEIF is the standard. HEIC is Apple's implementation of it, specifically the version using HEVC compression inside. Practically the two names refer to the same thing and files may carry either .heic or .heif. This converter accepts both.

Why your iPhone photos are HEIC in the first place

Since iOS 11, Apple has saved photos as HEIC by default rather than JPG. HEIC is Apple's implementation of the HEIF container, and it stores images compressed with HEVC , the same codec used for 4K video. The practical result is that a photo takes roughly half the storage of the equivalent JPG at similar visible quality.

That is a genuinely good trade on the phone itself. The problem starts the moment the file leaves the phone. Windows cannot open HEIC without an extension from the Microsoft Store. Most content management systems reject it on upload. Older versions of Photoshop, plenty of print shops, and a long tail of web forms simply do not recognise the format. So people end up needing a converter not because HEIC is bad, but because almost nothing outside Apple's ecosystem supports it.

Does converting HEIC to JPG lose quality?

Yes, a little, and it is worth understanding why rather than just worrying about it.

Both HEIC and JPG are lossy formats. Converting between them means decoding the HEIC back to raw pixels and then re-encoding those pixels as JPG. That second encode introduces its own compression, on top of compression that already happened. This is generation loss, and it is unavoidable when moving between any two lossy formats.

In practice it is rarely visible. At the 88% quality this tool defaults to, the difference on a normal photograph is almost impossible to see without pixel-peeping. Where it does show up is in large flat areas of gradient , skies, studio backdrops , which can develop faint banding. If you are converting for print or for archival purposes, push the quality slider to 100, or choose PNG instead.

When to pick PNG over JPG

  • Choose JPG for photographs, anything going on a website, anything you are emailing. Far smaller files, universally supported.
  • Choose PNG for screenshots, images with text or sharp edges, or anything you plan to edit further. PNG is lossless, so no additional generation loss is introduced , but the files will be considerably larger than the original HEIC.

What happens to the metadata

This is the part most converters do not tell you about. When a HEIC is decoded and re-encoded in a browser, EXIF metadata is generally not carried across. That means the converted JPG will typically lose the capture date, camera settings, and GPS coordinates that were embedded in the original.

Whether that is a problem or a feature depends entirely on what you are doing:

  • If you are posting photos publicly, stripping GPS coordinates is a privacy win. Photos taken at home carry your home address in them.
  • If you are archiving family photos, losing the capture date is a real loss, and your photo library will sort everything by conversion date instead. In that case keep the HEIC originals as well.

Either way, keep the originals until you have checked the converted files are what you expected. Conversion is not something to do destructively.
